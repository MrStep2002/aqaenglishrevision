Assessment Objectives

What are the Literature Assessment Objectives?

The assessment objectives are what teachers use to grade your responses. We are required to mark on what we can see and make a judgement on whether we can see evidence of it in the essay in front of us.

Examiners reward what you show you can do. There isn't just one correct interpretation, but your ideas need to answer the question and be supported by the text. A few well-developed points will usually show more understanding than a long list of unexplained possibilities. 

There are FOUR assessment objectives in Literature, which are detailed below, though they don't all apply to every essay:

Macbeth: AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4

A Christmas Carol: AO1, AO2 and AO3 

An Inspector Calls: AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4

Anthology poetry comparison: AO1, AO2 and AO3

Unseen Poetry: AO1 and AO2 for the 24-mark response; AO2 only for the 8-mark comparison

The 12/12/6 mark breakdown below applies to the 30-mark responses on your studied texts and anthology poetry. The unseen poetry questions have different mark allocations. These objectives work together: the same paragraph can develop an interpretation, analyse a writer's choices and explain a relevant context.

AO1: The Big Picture (12 Marks)

AO1 is about your response to the question: the interpretation you develop, how clearly you explain it, and the textual evidence you use to support it. Themes, plot and character development can all help you build that argument. You might explore:

Key moments of plot: for example - "The bit where Scrooge snuffs out the Ghost of Christmas Past suggests..." or "The fact that it is Macduff who kills Macbeth symbolises..." or "Gerald's treatment of Eva shows him to be..."

Key moments of Character: for example - "Scrooge's response to seeing Belle with her family shows him to be..." or "Macbeth's behaviour throughout Act 1 Scene 7 implies..." or "Mr Birling's behaviour throughout the opening of Act 1 shows him to be..."

Comparisons across the text: for example - "Comparing Scrooge's behaviour after he wakes up on Christmas morning with his behaviour during the opening of the novel shows that..." or "The fact that Macbeth order the death of Banquo shows that he has changed..." or "Sheila's behaviour here shows just how much she has grown since the opening of the play..."

For AO1 you are encouraged to use embedded quotes where you can, though where you can't remember the quote just make sure you reference the text as specifically as you can.

AO2: Language, Form and Structure (12 Marks)

AO2 is about how the writer's choices create meanings and effects. That includes language, form and structure: individual words and images, the kind of text being written, and the way its parts are arranged. Use relevant terminology where it helps explain your point, and show what the choice achieves rather than just naming a technique. A good guide to succeed here might be to think about the following:

Show you've understood the quote by embedding it - DON'T just say "At one point Macbeth says "We shall proceed no further in this business."" Instead, say "After a long speech exploring his reasons for not wanting to murder Duncan, Macbeth makes his feelings clear when he says to his wife "We shall proceed no further in this business."" Here, the second example shows I've understood where the quote comes in the play and shows I've really understood its meaning.

Zoom in on specific language to explore understanding - for example: "Here, the noun "business" suggests that Macbeth sees death almost as a part of his job - it's what Duncan paid him for, after all. Also the collective pronoun "we" makes it clear that Macbeth feels that he is speaking for the pair of them - they are embarking on this together and if one of them isn't doing it, neither of them should."

Look at form and structure as well as individual words - for example: "Dickens places Scrooge's joyful cries of 'Hallo! Whoop! Hallo here!' at the end of the novella, where they contrast with his earlier 'Bah! Humbug!' This change in his voice makes his transformation audible to the reader."

AO3: Context: The Text and Its World (6 Marks)

AO3 asks you to connect the text with the contexts in which it was written. That might mean the social conditions, beliefs, literary traditions or political debates that help explain a writer's choices. For example, you could connect Scrooge's comments about the workhouses with Victorian attitudes towards poverty, and explain how Dickens challenges those attitudes through the story.

A modern comparison can help you think about a text's relevance, but it should develop your reading of the text and its contexts. Simply mentioning a current event or adding a fact about the writer's life does not explain the connection.

How to Include Context:
Bring context into your argument where it helps explain a particular detail or idea. Instead of adding a separate paragraph of historical facts, show how a relevant belief or condition shapes what the writer presents. Be specific about the audience you mean, and support claims about how readers or audiences might respond with evidence from the text.

AO4: Vocabulary, Sentences, Spelling and Punctuation (4 Marks)

There are 4 additional AO4 marks on the Shakespeare response in Paper 1 and the modern-text response in Paper 2. For the texts covered here, that means Macbeth and An Inspector Calls. There are no separate AO4 marks on A Christmas Carol or the poetry responses.

Choose vocabulary and sentence structures that express your ideas clearly and precisely, and use accurate spelling and punctuation. An unfamiliar or elaborate word is only useful if it helps you say what you mean.

AQA's Assessment Objectives

The explanations above are a guide to using the objectives in your writing. You can read AQA's official assessment objectives and qualification weightings in the Literature specification.
