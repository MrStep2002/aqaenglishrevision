How to Revise

IMPORTANT NOTE:
There is a LOT of text on this site. There's lots to read... this is partly because learning to read is really important and partly because reading is THE BEST way to improve your writing. 

Reading is NEVER a waste of time,
and reading revision materials, is ALWAYS useful.

With English, you sit two GCSEs - Language and Literature. For each GCSE you have two exams, so there are four exams in all.

Language:
Each language paper has the same structure:

  • Four reading questions
  • One extended writing task

Paper 1 is based on one fiction extract, followed by a choice of descriptive or narrative writing. From summer 2026, the narrative option asks for the opening of a story. Paper 2 is based on two linked non-fiction extracts, followed by a task asking you to present a viewpoint. Both Language exams are 1 hour 45 minutes long.

Literature:
The papers cover the texts and poetry you have studied in school:

  • Paper 1: Shakespeare and the 19th-century novel. You answer one question on your Shakespeare play, such as Macbeth, and one on your novel, such as A Christmas Carol. Each question asks you to write about an extract and the text as a whole. This exam is 1 hour 45 minutes long.
  • Paper 2: Modern texts and poetry. You answer one question on your modern text, such as An Inspector Calls; one question comparing a printed anthology poem with another poem you recall from your studied cluster; and two unseen poetry questions. This exam is 2 hours 15 minutes long.

Literature exams are closed book: you cannot take your texts into the exam. The Paper 1 extracts, the named anthology poem and the unseen poems are printed on the paper; you draw on your memory for the wider texts and the second anthology poem.

Exam information checked November 2026. See AQA's Literature specification, Language specification and Language updates from summer 2026.

How to prepare for Literature: Planning

Planning for a literature exam is as easy as 1, 2, 3:

Know the text:
This includes being so comfortable with the plot you could recount it to a friend - just means you can say what it's about. There's a section about plot for each of the key texts here.
Also, you'll need to know the key characters well - what is their role in the story. There's a bit about this for each text as well.
And finally, you'll need to know what key themes appear in the text - each theme has a section.

Get comfortable with key quotes:
When the new GCSE came out a lot of people were worried that students would just spend all their time learning quotes, but you DO NOT have to do this. However, you will need to get comfortable with some of the key things that people say as you're going to use them to put together your argument.

Plan your essays:
Don't leave it until exam day to work out what you think about a text. For each text there's a page called Revision Essay Titles with a range of questions you can prepare for. Use them to develop arguments about characters and themes, and practise choosing evidence to support those arguments. On the day, read the exact question carefully and adapt your ideas to it: use your prepared plans to help you choose relevant ideas and evidence.

Planning an essay...

Planning is a useful way to prepare for Literature. You don't know what question will come up on the day, but practising a range of questions helps you build a bank of ideas and evidence that you can draw on.

I've made a list of ten possible questions that might come up for A Christmas Carol, Macbeth and An Inspector Calls (you'll find them by clicking on those links, or by visiting that section of the site.)

Though planning 10 essays might seem a lot, if you're smart it needn't be that hard at all. For example, a paragraph on Mr Birling's role as a capitalist might come up in an essay about the political message of the play, or an essay about Mr Birling - or even an essay about the inspector. 

For each plan you should have:

  • A possible opening paragraph, ready to adapt to the wording of the question
  • A summary of what will go into each paragraph
  • A list of which key quotes you'll look at in detail, and some notes about them

Everything you'll need for each essay will be on the site, so use your essay planning as a reason to go digging around the site.

Important Note about extracts:
For the Shakespeare and 19th-century novel questions, including Macbeth and A Christmas Carol, you must write about both the printed extract and the text as a whole. Read the extract carefully, choose details that help answer the question, and connect them to relevant moments elsewhere in the text. You can discuss the extract first or weave it into your wider argument; either way, make sure your answer covers both parts of the task.

What about Unseen poetry?
Because the Unseen poetry part of the exam is... well, UNSEEN, you can't prepare an answer to a particular poem beforehand. I've written some sample answers and given you some pieces to practise with, so have a go with them. The first question is worth 24 marks and asks you to analyse one poem. The second is worth 8 marks and asks you to compare how the poets use methods in that poem and a second one. For the comparison, explain how the writing creates similar or different effects, rather than just listing similarities in subject matter.

To revise for literature, read, revisit the text, and plan answers to a range of questions. Build up ideas you understand well enough to adapt, then choose the ones that actually answer the question in front of you.

How to prepare for Language: Practice

Both the language papers are unseen, which means it's difficult to plan an answer beforehand. This doesn't mean you can't revise though. The key is this:

Make sure you know exactly what you have to do for each question and prepare a simple revision card with a clear set of instructions for yourself - you can visit the Language paper section of the website for some guidance on how to do this.

Practise writing your own answers using the examples in the Language section of the website.

What about creative writing?

There are SO many different ways you can approach a piece of creative writing that it's very difficult to give any really worthwhile advice. It's worth reading some flash fiction and descriptive writing to give you an idea of what's achievable, and it might be worth practicing with a couple of the structures to see if you can find one that resonates with you. 

However, the basic rules of grammar won't change and so it's definitely worth getting to grips with them. Although (officially) SPAG is only worth 16 out of 40 marks, the reality is that SPAG and style are far more closely linked than we like to think and if your SPAG improves then the mark out of 24 will improve as well.

So, in a nutshell, this is what I'd do to prepare for creative writing:

  • Visit the short story, descriptive and non-fiction sections of the website and get a good idea of what's possible. 
  • Find a couple of ideas that connect with you and have a practice with them either by planning or writing a story along those lines
  • Make sure you're confident with all the major punctuation points and common spelling errors, and plan to show you understand how to use them on the day
