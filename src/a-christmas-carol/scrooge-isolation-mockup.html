Charles Dickens · Stave OneThe extract

Oh! But he was a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone, Scrooge! a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner! Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster. The cold within him froze his old features, nipped his pointed nose, shrivelled his cheek, stiffened his gait; made his eyes red, his thin lips blue; and spoke out shrewdly in his grating voice. A frosty rime was on his head, and on his eyebrows, and his wiry chin. He carried his own low temperature always about with him; he iced his office in the dog-days; and didn’t thaw it one degree at Christmas.

External heat and cold had little influence on Scrooge. No warmth could warm, no wintry weather chill him. No wind that blew was bitterer than he, no falling snow was more intent upon its purpose, no pelting rain less open to entreaty. Foul weather didn’t know where to have him. The heaviest rain, and snow, and hail, and sleet, could boast of the advantage over him in only one respect. They often “came down” handsomely, and Scrooge never did.

Nobody ever stopped him in the street to say, with gladsome looks, “My dear Scrooge, how are you? When will you come to see me?” No beggars implored him to bestow a trifle, no children asked him what it was o’clock, no man or woman ever once in all his life inquired the way to such and such a place, of Scrooge. Even the blind men’s dogs appeared to know him; and when they saw him coming on, would tug their owners into doorways and up courts; and then would wag their tails as though they said, “No eye at all is better than an evil eye, dark master!”

But what did Scrooge care! It was the very thing he liked. To edge his way along the crowded paths of life, warning all human sympathy to keep its distance, was what the knowing ones call “nuts” to Scrooge.

Read the extract annotations
Tight-fisted hand at the grindstone
A tight fist suggests someone unwilling to let go of money. The grindstone brings work to mind, so Dickens introduces Scrooge through the way he does business.
Squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous
The list piles up forceful actions associated with taking and holding on. “Covetous” means eager to possess what others have, and “sinner” turns greed into a moral failing.
Hard and sharp as flint
Flint can produce sparks when struck with steel. Dickens twists the simile: Scrooge has the stone’s hardness, but nobody has ever drawn a spark of generosity from him.
Solitary as an oyster
An oyster shuts itself inside a hard shell. The repeated ‘s’ sounds link “secret,” “self-contained” and “solitary,” presenting Scrooge as someone who keeps others out. Could the shell also hint at something worth finding inside?
The cold within him froze his old features
The cold starts “within” Scrooge and changes his appearance. Dickens makes emotional coldness visible in his face, posture and voice.
Dog-days and Christmas
The “dog-days” are the hottest days of summer. Even summer heat and the goodwill associated with Christmas fail to thaw him. This sets up a contrast with the warmth he shows by the end.
No wind that blew was bitterer than he
“Bitter” can describe harsh weather or a resentful person. Dickens uses both senses and makes Scrooge outdo even the wind.
Came down handsomely
The weather literally comes down as rain, snow or hail. To “come down handsomely” can also mean to give generously. The joke makes even bad weather seem more charitable than Scrooge.
Nobody ever stopped him
“Nobody,” “no beggars,” “no children” and “no man or woman” build a picture of isolation. Even an ordinary greeting or a request for directions is missing from his life.
No eye at all is better than an evil eye
The narrator imagines the guide dogs making a joke about sight. They steer away from Scrooge as if his hostile look could do harm, exaggerating how thoroughly people avoid him.
Warning all human sympathy to keep its distance
Scrooge actively keeps kindness and fellow feeling away. “Nuts” here means something he enjoys, so his isolation is presented as a choice.

In A Christmas Carol, Dickens presents Scrooge’s isolation as a barrier that damages both his own life and the lives of others. The opening extract introduces a man who seems to enjoy shutting the world out, but the spirits reveal the pain beneath this apparent independence and the future it could produce. Through his eventual return to the community, Dickens challenges a wealthy person’s refusal of social responsibility and celebrates compassion as a means of change.

In the extract, Scrooge’s isolation initially appears to be something he actively maintains. Dickens describes him as “secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster”. The simile suggests a creature enclosed within a hard shell: Scrooge protects himself from contact, but that protection also cuts him off from human relationships. The repeated conjunction “and” accumulates descriptions of withdrawal, making his separation seem thorough. Similarly, the opening description of a “tight-fisted hand” and the actions “squeezing” and “clutching” connect his closed character to his handling of money. He holds on to his possessions so tightly that he seems unable to reach out to anyone else. Dickens therefore makes greed and loneliness parts of the same problem.

Dickens also makes Scrooge’s emotional separation visible through the extract’s persistent cold imagery. The narrator explains that “the cold within him froze his old features”, while even flint has never produced “generous fire” from him. The word “within” locates the source of this coldness inside Scrooge: his surroundings do not simply make him miserable; his own attitude freezes the world around him. The adjective “generous” gives fire a human quality, associating warmth with the kindness he withholds. At Christmas, a festival associated with Christian charity and goodwill, this refusal of warmth becomes especially disturbing. The narrator’s label “old sinner” similarly presents his behaviour as a moral failing, preparing for a story of repentance and renewal.

As the extract develops, Dickens moves from Scrooge’s appearance to the everyday relationships missing from his life. “Nobody ever stopped him”, and the repeated negatives extend to “no beggars”, “no children” and “no man or woman”. The sequence excludes people of different ages and circumstances, suggesting that he belongs to no part of the community. Even ordinary conversation and requests for directions are absent. Yet the narrator insists that this is “the very thing he liked”. The contrast between the “crowded paths of life” and his demand that sympathy “keep its distance” makes his isolation particularly striking: people surround him, but he refuses connection. His loneliness is therefore also an act of rejection.

However, the wider novella complicates this first impression by revealing that Scrooge has also suffered from being excluded. In Stave Two, the Ghost of Christmas Past describes him as “a solitary child, neglected by his friends”. The repetition of “solitary” links the vulnerable child to the adult oyster, but “neglected” shifts responsibility towards those who left him alone. Dickens’s return to the past encourages the reader to reconsider a character who initially seemed merely unpleasant. Scrooge sobs at the memory and wishes he had given something to the boy who sang at his door. This movement from remembering pain to recognising another child’s need suggests that sympathy can break the pattern of isolation. His past helps explain his hardness without making his present cruelty inevitable.

Scrooge’s separation also represents a wider refusal to recognise obligations towards the poor. Elsewhere in Stave One, he answers the charity collectors by asking, “Are there no workhouses?” The workhouse system strengthened by the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act made relief deliberately deterrent. Against this background, Scrooge’s question suggests that institutional provision excuses him from personal compassion. Marley challenges that narrow outlook when he declares, “Mankind was my business.” By giving “business” a moral meaning beyond profit, Dickens suggests that human beings are bound to one another. The man who keeps sympathy at a distance must learn that other people’s welfare concerns him too.

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come finally reveals the destination of a life built on separation. The businessmen discuss Scrooge’s death with indifference, while the Cratchits grieve deeply for Tiny Tim. This juxtaposition exposes the difference between having money and mattering to others. The absence of affection at Scrooge’s death becomes the frightening outcome of the opening’s repeated negatives. However, the vision is a warning of what could happen, so Dickens uses fear to create an opportunity for change.

By the ending, Dickens reverses the extract’s exclusions and shows Scrooge choosing involvement. At the ending, the narrator describes how Scrooge “patted children on the head, and questioned beggars”; he also becomes a “second father” to Tiny Tim. The return of children and beggars transforms the earlier pattern of avoidance into active contact. The familial phrase “second father” gives Scrooge a lasting relationship and a responsibility that his money can help him fulfil. His redemption depends on putting charity and fellow feeling into practice: he joins Fred’s celebration and supports Bob’s family. The contrast with the opening suggests that the barrier around him was changeable, and that belonging requires him to offer the sympathy he once rejected.

Ultimately, Dickens presents isolation as both a private loss and a failure of responsibility towards others. By moving from the closed oyster to the second father, the novella turns a portrait of exclusion into a story of renewed connection. Scrooge’s change offers Dickens’s readers the hopeful possibility that a more compassionate society begins with recognising the claims other people have upon us.

Looking at the response

Examiner-style comments

Draft teaching commentary for us to refine; these are not official AQA examiner comments.

Overall

A sustained line of argument

The response keeps returning to a clear interpretation: Scrooge’s isolation is personally damaging and socially irresponsible, but change remains possible. The discussion of the wider novella grows from the printed extract.

AO1

Relevant references, used purposefully

Evidence from the opening, Scrooge’s childhood, Marley’s warning and the ending supports the same developing argument. The response also recognises a complication: being neglected helps explain Scrooge’s behaviour without excusing his cruelty.

AO2

Analysis moves between words and the whole text

The reading of the oyster, cold imagery and repeated negatives explains how Dickens creates meanings. Connecting the children and beggars at the beginning and end is a particularly useful structural observation.

AO3

Context informs the interpretation

Christian ideas of charity and redemption, and the debate about responsibility for the poor, are linked to specific details. The Poor Law reference helps explain the attitude behind Scrooge’s question about workhouses.

Next step

Explore the narrator’s humour more closely

The extract’s imagined guide dogs and the joke about the weather could support a richer discussion of narrative voice. How does making Scrooge ridiculous affect our response to his isolation? This would extend the existing analysis of Dickens’s methods.
