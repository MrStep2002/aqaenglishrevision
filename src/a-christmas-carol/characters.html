Characters

A Christmas Carol is an interesting piece when it comes to characters. They’re all great fun in their own way, and even the ones who are given very little time on the page are clear and vivid. But it’s important to remember that we feel like we know them so quickly because they’re all caricatures rather than actual people. This means that they’re all about as realistic as someone from an episode of Friends, but it also means that Dickens can bring life to them in a very small space of time.

Belle is simply beautiful and Scrooge loved her, Fezziwig is a great guy and loads of fun, Bob is poor but a wonderful father, etc… They’re not real people, they’re archetypes that we recognise instantly and this allows us to focus on the only real character that matters: Scrooge.

The fact that they’re not realistic has led to some criticisms of the book. But it was written to be more of a Christmas blockbuster than a “literary classic.” The book is fun and the characters reflect this. They’re not realistic, but they’re not supposed to be.

However, there is something interesting in the fact that Dickens managed to take these caricatures and cliches and turn them into something that has real value. In many ways, his commentary about life - that bad people have learnt to be bad by being neglected, and can learn to be good once they realise the value of love - only works because the characters are all so simplistically drawn. The fact is that Scrooge himself is actually quite a complex character, and the book says some complex things about the human condition. And the fact that Dickens does this using caricatures shouldn’t detract from the profundity of the message.

The Main Characters

Ebenezer Scrooge
Scrooge is the owner of a counting-house, which means he's the Victorian version of a loan shark: he lends money to poor people at ridiculously high levels of interest and then puts them in prison if they can't pay. Throughout the story he learns that it feels good to help people rather than make money out of them, and that happiness hasn’t got anything to do with money anyway. His lesson is a huge relief for everyone else, and, as it happens, Scrooge is a lot happier about it too!

Scrooge is the focus of the whole book and everything that happens to all the characters is only important in relation to Scrooge. In many ways he’s a cliche of a character - the old miser is a literary archetype that goes way back. However, the way that Dickens deals with him is interesting: he is punished for his behaviour, but not just by seeing how badly he treated others, but by seeing how damaging his behaviour has been to himself. In a funny way, he learns to be good not to help other people but to help himself.

Though this might seem like a selfish reason for being good, it is also, perhaps, one of the most profound pieces of wisdom there is.

Bob Cratchit
Bob Cratchit is Scrooge's hardworking help. He works hard for Scrooge, earns a pittance, and supports a large family on the back of it. Throughout the story, Bob's chipper nature teaches Scrooge important things about the real value of money. Bob broke but happy; Scrooge is minted but miserable.

Within this, however, there is a clear criticism of the presentation of the Cratchits: they created this image of the miserable rich and the happy poor. Some people, even today, fall back on this attitude: “They may be poor, but look at them all with their working class songs and their gravy on their chips and them sitting around and chatting to each other! They don’t need money to be happy, which is why I’m going to keep all my money.” Equally, people look at the rich and say “they may be rich, but they’re not happy.” TV shows like Succession play off this same image.

There is some truth in the image, but it’s simplistic and doesn’t defend the current situation with wealth inequality. The truth is that, beyond a certain point, being rich doesn’t make you happier (there have been studies that have proven this.) But being poor, beyond a certain point, introduces such a level of struggle that it is hard to imaging happiness existing alongside it.

In short: I’m glad the Cratchits were happy, but that doesn’t excuse keeping them in poverty!

Jacob Marley

Jacob was Scrooge's partner - equally miserly, equally horrible, BUT Jacob died first which meant that Jacob learnt the real cost of what they'd done. At the beginning of the story, Jacob's ghost arrives and tells Scrooge what a terrible fate awaits him if he doesn't change.

Marley’s fate is interesting. Dickens was a Christian and there is something of the Christian value system embedded in his afterlife experience: the idea of being stuck in purgatory, which was a sort of waiting room before the afterlife proper is definitely there.

But it’s a lot deeper than that as well. Marley has almost returned to a state of purity; a non-judgemental existence that means he can see the world for what it is; and it is this understanding that leads him to want to help other people, but his death has made that impossible.

In this world, there is no “judgement” after death, there is no God who punishes those who have done wrong. They are given the enlightenment of divine understanding and then simply left to face the world they created. In many ways, they judge themselves which is, I think, a far more profound and more powerful way of enforcing judgement.

The Spirits

The Ghost of Christmas Past
The first spirit to visit Scrooge, is a curious apparition that might be a candle, which is a symbol of memories, or might be a kind of angel. This being takes Scrooge on a tour of Christmases in his past. Here, Scrooge is reminded of the person he used to be, and understands all that he lost. This recollects Marley’s situation where it is self-judgement that brings about change.

Also, there is a lot of the psychoanalyst about this ghost. The way it can put its hat on and bury the memories almost like returning them to the subconscious. In many ways, Scrooge’s process in this stave is the process of therapy: he faces his past, including all the memories he buried as they were just too painful; he learns their lessons and then snuffs out their light, leaves behind their influence, heals the pain they left him with and moves on from his past.

The Ghost of Christmas Present

The second spirit to visit Scrooge is a majestic giant clad in a green robe - very similar to the Pagan God The Green Man (and father Christmas, as it happens.) He escorts Scrooge on a tour of his contemporaries' holiday celebrations and reminds Scrooge that there is still joy taking place in his world today.

He is a joyous soul, who was supposed to remind us that on this day we should all be happy and take part in celebrations that will bring us together. However, at the end of the stave he reveals two starving children that are clearly forgotten. The truth is that we love to celebrate, but, in doing so, we too often tend to ignore things that bring us down. Who wants to be depressed on Christmas Day?

These children still exist, they exist all year, but their real horror is only exposed when it is contrasted with the luxury of a Christmas celebration for those who have it all.

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come

The third and final spirit to visit Scrooge is a silent phantom clad in a hooded black robe - he's basically the Grim Reaper (also known simply as Death).

This spirit shows Scrooge a truth that he has always known: everybody hates him! Even the people he thought liked him, like the banker with whom Scrooge had good business, hated him. Even his servants, who he paid, hated him. Everyone hated him!

The truth is that everyone was just a business to Scrooge. And if you treat everyone that way, they’ll treat you that way back. This understanding serves as the final catalyst Scrooge needed to change.

The Ensemble

Tiny Tim

Bob Cratchit's young son is crippled - it was probably polio - but he's endlessly optimistic. He's a kind of benchmark against who we should set our standards: basically, if you're not the crippled son of a poor Victorian, get over yourself and enjoy life!

Again, though, Tim can be used as a means to justify the vision of the happy poor: with a benchmark as low as Tiny Tim, everyone should be happy and accept their lot in life. But this doesn’t excuse the treatment that a lot of people get or mean that the poor, then or today, should just shut up and accept their lot.

Fred

Fred is another happy soul. He embodies the spirit that Dickens wanted to imbue us all with and he is never better surmised than with his famous speech, that’s worth reading again:

“Christmas is a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely, and to think of people below them as if they really were fellow-passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on other journeys. And therefore, uncle, though it has never put a scrap of gold or silver in my pocket, I believe that it has done me good, and will do me good; and I say, God bless it!”

That speech was what Dickens called his Carol Philosophy and it sums up Fred!

(Though there’s also an argument that he’s only nice to his uncle as he’s hoping to get written into the will, but that’s a little cynical of me and is never even hinted at in the book!)

Fezziwig
Dickens knew how to name his characters and Fezziwig is every bit as fun as his name makes him sound! He's Scrooge's old boss and he was a much better boss than Scrooge!

His involvement in the book can be summed up thusly: He turns up, hosts a rave, then disappears again.

A true legend, in any age!

Belle
Scrooge really loved Belle, but she could see that he only loved money. So she left him, and the Ghost of Christmas Past makes Scrooge see just how happy she became!

There is a case for saying that Scrooge was so terrified of poverty, for him and her, that he lost himself in the midst of trying to protect her from it. Poverty was deadly, as Scrooge well knew, and the man was supposed to provide for the whole family in those days so you could argue that Scrooge only did what he did to protect her.

In the scene where they part he says: “There is nothing on which [the world] is so hard as poverty; and there is nothing it professes to condemn with such severity as the pursuit of wealth!” This does well to sum up the reasons why Scrooge became such a miser - over-compensation for his fear of poverty.

Maybe he was just trying to protect her… but a good marriage needs to be built on more than protection from poverty, as Belle well knew.

Fan
Scrooge's sister and Fred's mother. In Scrooge's vision of Christmases past, Fanny saved him from being alone at school.

She also utters a line that should change our vision on what happened to Scrooge:

“Father is so much kinder than he used to be, that home’s like Heaven! He spoke so gently to me one dear night when I was going to bed, that I was not afraid to ask him once more if you might come home.”

Father is kinder than he used to be… she wasn’t afraid anymore. Both of these things suggest that Scrooge’s father was a notorious tyrant and it’s possible that standing up to him was one of the reasons why Scrooge was condemned to spend his early Christmases alone.

In end, did Scrooge just do what Freud tells us we all do, and become his father? Quite possibly, and it’s little details like this that manage to turn Scrooge from a cliche into a genuinely complex and interesting character.

Dick Wilkins
If Fanny saved Scrooge when he was young, it was Dick that saved him later on. Dick was Scrooge's best friend while he started work, and this was the case right up until the moment that Scrooge and Belle-end. And that's why hyphens are important.

The Portly Gentlemen

Portly means large, rotund, well built. Dickens doesn't give these guys names, he just mentions the fact that they're fat. These characters are basically called "The Fat Guys." Dickens does this to highlight the fact that, although they were collecting for charity, they certainly weren't suffering for their cause.

It’s just Scrooge who has to answer for society’s ills. We are all a part of the problem, even people who get around to doing something decent - once a year, just to be safe.

They remind me of the t-shirt that says: “Look busy! Jesus is coming!”

Mrs. Cratchit

It's worth noting that, other than Belle, all the female characters in this book are only known by their husbands name. It was a sign of the times, but it's worth mentioning.

It’s also worth mentioning that Mrs Cratchit is actually quite a spirited and expressive woman. She isn’t afraid of saying what she thinks about Scrooge and this leads to a lovely discussion over the dining table about Scrooge’s role in their family’s life.
